Research

My research sits at the intersection of supramolecular chemistry, luminescent nanomaterials, and analytical spectroscopy — from designing new phosphorescent solid-state materials to developing the methods needed to characterize them.

PhD research — Long-lived luminescence in supramolecular constructs

University of Copenhagen, Nano-Science Center, 2021–2024. Supervisors: Prof. Bo Wegge Laursen (Copenhagen), Prof. Amar H. Flood (Indiana University).

My PhD explored how to generate long-lived, room-temperature phosphorescence from small organic molecules by controlling their packing in the solid state — work centered on SMILES (Small-Molecule Ionic Isolation Lattices), where a host anion (cyanostar) isolates emissive dye molecules from each other and from oxygen, suppressing non-radiative decay and quenching.

Project 1 — Triplet state of cyanostar

Using phosphorescence quenching studies (Stern-Volmer analysis) combined with computational simulation, we measured the triplet energies of the parent cyanostar host and its 2:1 anion complex (around PF₆⁻) at 1.96 eV and 2.02 eV respectively — establishing the energetics needed to design efficient triplet energy transfer into the SMILES framework.

Project 2 — Translating molecular phosphorescence to solid emitters

By packing platinum-based phosphorescent dyes (PtTA compounds) alternately with cyanostar/anion complexes in the solid state, we reproduced cryogenic-like rigidity at room temperature. This SMILES stacking effect boosted phosphorescence lifetime substantially over solution and aggregate references, and enabled fabrication of SMILES nanoparticles emitting near 690 nm with millisecond-scale lifetimes — relevant for time-gated bioimaging.

Project 3 & 4 — Boosting triplet emission & activating charge-transfer emission

Extending the SMILES approach to porphyrin-based emitters (Por4) and to systems with charge-transfer character, showing the strategy generalizes beyond a single dye class and can be used to tune both emission wavelength and excited-state lifetime.

This work involved close collaboration with groups in Sweden (Chalmers) and the USA (Indiana University), and access to advanced facilities including MAX IV and ISBUC. The full thesis, "Exploring Long-Lived Luminescence in Supramolecular Constructs," is available here.

Master's research — Polysiloxane-functionalized carbon dots

Tianjin University, School of Chemical Engineering and Technology, 2017–2020. Advisor: Prof. Yakai Feng.

My master's work focused on carbon dots (CDs) — small, biocompatible carbon-based nanomaterials with tunable photoluminescence but a tendency to self-quench. I prepared polysiloxane-functionalized CDs via a solvothermal reaction between citric acid and aminopropyl-containing polysiloxanes, producing particles from 2.2–6.4 nm in size.

Tuning the aminopropyl content shifted the UV-Vis absorption and fluorescence emission of the resulting CDs, with the highest fluorescence quantum yields observed at 50–75% aminopropyl content. The polysiloxane shell also improved colloidal stability — a strategy later extended to an industrial protocol for fluorescently tracking silicone-oil leakage in thermally conductive pads (developed during an R&D role at Laird Ltd.), which led to a granted patent.
