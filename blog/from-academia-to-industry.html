From the bench to business innovation: what my PhD actually prepared me for

This is a starter/placeholder post — edit or replace it with your own words.

Earlier this year I started as a Senior Scientist in FOSS's Business Innovation department — focused on analytics development, chemometrics, and spectroscopic instrumental support. It's a bit of a departure from where I spent the last several years: measuring the phosphorescence lifetimes of weakly emissive crystals in a basement lab at the University of Copenhagen.

On paper, "supramolecular photophysics" and "business innovation" don't look like they belong on the same CV. But a few things carried over more directly than I expected:

  • Working with weak, messy signals. Most of my PhD was spent coaxing usable data out of low-signal, non-ideal systems — exactly the kind of precision that method development and QC work in industry demands.
  • Translating science into value. Writing a thesis chapter and pitching a project to a commercial stakeholder are different exercises, but both require finding the version of the story that resonates with the person in front of you.
  • Working across disciplines. Bridging chemistry, physics, and materials science during my PhD turned out to be good practice for now bridging science and commercial priorities.

I'll be using this space to write more about spectroscopy, nanomaterials, and what the academia-to-industry transition actually looks like in practice — not the polished LinkedIn version.
